# Was Origen condemned? A survey of the evidence and the scholarship

*Prepared 2026-10-15 for the Origen reading-list project. The question is the reader's:*
*was Origen of Alexandria actually condemned as a heretic; if so by whom, when, with what authority, and was it justified; if not Origen himself, what was condemned; and was universal salvation (apokatastasis) as such condemned?*

This is a survey of what others have argued, not a verdict. Where the evidence settles a point, the report says so. Where it doesn't, it sets the positions side by side and names who holds them. Every claim is listed with its source and check status in [claims.md](claims.md), and the bias review is in [balance-audit.md](balance-audit.md).

---

## The short answer

1. **In his lifetime**, Origen was censured by his own bishop, Demetrius of Alexandria, in two synods around 231. The surviving accounts make the grievance chiefly his ordination at Caesarea without Demetrius's leave, not his doctrine. Other churches, Palestine above all, ignored the sentence.
2. **Around 400**, a synod under Theophilus of Alexandria condemned Origen and his books, and Pope Anastasius I joined in. This was a regional act driven as much by church politics as by doctrine, and universal salvation was not the charge that carried it.
3. **In 543**, Justinian issued a treatise against Origen ending in ten anathemas. The ninth condemns the view that the punishment of demons and the wicked is temporary and ends in restoration (*apokatastasis*), and the last anathematizes Origen by name. A home synod at Constantinople endorsed it. Its authority was imperial and local.
4. **In 553**, fifteen anathemas against an Origenist system were signed by the bishops gathered at Constantinople. The view most historians now take is that they signed them *before the council formally opened*, so the anathemas are not in its Acts. Origen's name *is* in the council's eleventh anathema, in a list of heretics. Whether it was there from the start is disputed.
5. **Afterwards**, later councils (Constantinople III, 680–81; Nicaea II, 787) speak of the fifth council as having met "against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius". For the Byzantine and later Orthodox tradition, Origen counted as condemned.
6. **What the 553 texts describe** is a sixth-century system (pre-existent minds, a cosmic Christ-*nous*, a final dissolution of bodies and names) that most specialists trace to Evagrius of Pontus and his Palestinian followers rather than to Origen's own writings.
7. **Whether apokatastasis as such was condemned** is the live dispute today. One side reads the 543 ninth anathema and the 553 first anathema as condemning universal salvation outright. The other reads them as condemning a version tied to pre-existence and a return to a bodiless beginning, which would leave the universalism of Gregory of Nyssa (never condemned, and venerated as a saint) untouched.

## 1. Origen's life, and the first censure

Origen was born in Alexandria in 185 or 186. His father Leonides was beheaded in the persecution of 202 under Septimius Severus, when Origen was not yet seventeen. Most of what we know comes from Book VI of Eusebius's *Church History*, which scholars read with care: it is a defence written by an admirer, built in part on the lost *Apology for Origen* that Pamphilus wrote with Eusebius.

He taught in Alexandria for some twenty years. Eusebius's story that he castrated himself after reading Matthew 19:12 literally is disputed. Trigg accepts it. McGuckin calls it "hardly credible", and Chadwick suggests it was gossip from Origen's enemies. Origen himself condemns the literal reading of that verse in his *Commentary on Matthew*.

Around 231, passing through Caesarea on church business, Origen was ordained presbyter by Theoctistus of Caesarea (with Alexander of Jerusalem). Demetrius treated the ordination as a breach of his authority and held two synods. Our only account of what they did is a brief notice in Photius's *Bibliotheca* summarising the lost Pamphilus–Eusebius *Apology*. On that account, the first synod barred Origen from Alexandria and the second deprived him of his priesthood. Origen settled in Caesarea, where he worked for the rest of his life, including on the Hexapla, his six-column edition of the Hebrew Bible.

The doctrinal charge in this first round was the salvation of the devil. In a letter preserved by Rufinus (*On the Falsification of the Books of Origen*, 6), Origen denied ever teaching it. Readers differ on how far that denial settles his view of the end. Crouzel, and Kimel following him, read the episode as essentially disciplinary. Jenkins (as Kimel reports him) counts Demetrius's censure among the condemnations.

Origen was imprisoned and tortured under Decius in 250. He survived the persecution but died within a few years, aged sixty-nine according to Eusebius, around 253 or 254. The tradition that he died at Tyre goes back to Jerome.

**What the first censure was:** a local, largely disciplinary sentence by one bishop, which Palestine and Arabia did not accept. It did not make Origen a heretic in the eyes of the wider church, which went on reading him.

## 2. The first Origenist controversy (c. 375–403)

The case against Origen in the fourth century was made first by Epiphanius of Salamis in the *Ancoratus* (375) and the *Panarion* (376, heresy 64). His charges were subordinationism in the Trinity, allegorical excess, the pre-existence of souls and a spiritualised resurrection. Universal salvation was not at the centre of it. (Earlier, Methodius of Olympus had attacked Origen's view of the resurrection body, and Pamphilus and Eusebius had written their *Apology* in reply.)

The quarrel then spread through Palestine. John of Jerusalem refused Epiphanius's demands, holding that a man long dead could not be retroactively condemned as a heretic. Jerome, once Origen's translator, turned against him; Rufinus defended him. In 397–98 Rufinus published a Latin translation of *On First Principles* that softened passages he thought had been corrupted, and the two men's exchange of apologies that followed is the most bitter literary feud of the period.

In Egypt the controversy took a political shape. In 399–400 Theophilus of Alexandria turned against the Origenist monks of Nitria, and a synod at Alexandria in 400 condemned Origen and his followers. Theophilus called Origen the "hydra of all heresies". Pope Anastasius I accepted the condemnation: in a letter to John of Jerusalem concerning Rufinus (c. 401) he says he does not know who Origen was and does not wish to, but that his writings undermine the apostles' faith, and he refers to an imperial decree of Arcadius and Honorius against reading them. Theophilus drove out the monks known as the Tall Brothers. When John Chrysostom took them in at Constantinople, Theophilus pursued him, and Chrysostom was deposed at the Synod of the Oak in 403.

Elizabeth A. Clark's *The Origenist Controversy: The Cultural Construction of an Early Christian Debate* (1992) is the standard study of this round. Her argument is that Origen's speculative theology was not the only thing at stake: "Origen" became a code word for complaints that weren't theological at all, about asceticism, the body, and the relation of practice to theory.

**What this round settled:** Origen was condemned by a regional synod and by the Roman bishop, and from about 400 his name was suspect in much of the church. Kimel points out that neither Epiphanius nor Theophilus made universal salvation the decisive charge, and argues that it was still held to be within the bounds of opinion at the end of the fourth century. On the other side, Jenkins treats these condemnations as valid and part of the case.

## 3. The sixth-century documents

### The background: Palestinian Origenism

In the early sixth century, monks of the New Lavra in Palestine developed a speculative system drawing on Origen and, above all, on Evagrius of Pontus (d. 399). They split into two parties: the *Isochristoi*, who held that in the end all minds would become equal to Christ, and the *Protoktistoi*, who made Christ's soul the first created mind. Our main narrative source is Cyril of Scythopolis's *Lives* of the Palestinian monks, written from the anti-Origenist side. Daniël Hombergen's *The Second Origenist Controversy* (2001) argues that Cyril is a partisan source who seriously contradicts other evidence, and that what we call "Origenism" here is partly his construction.

### 543: Justinian's edict and the home synod

Prompted by anti-Origenist monks from the Great Lavra of St Sabas, and with the papal envoy Pelagius involved, Justinian wrote a long treatise against Origen, addressed to Patriarch Menas, ending in ten anathemas. A *synodos endemousa* (the standing synod of bishops resident in the capital) endorsed it, and Pope Vigilius and the other patriarchs subscribed. Price notes that the canons were issued as an imperial decree, so their authority was the emperor's rather than a council's.

The anathemas (NPNF II.14) condemn, in order: the pre-existence of souls; the pre-existence of the Lord's soul; the formation of Christ's body before its union with the Word; the Word becoming a cherub among cherubim; spherical resurrection bodies; ensouled heavens and stars; a future crucifixion of Christ for demons; a limit to God's power; and, in the ninth:

> "If anyone says or thinks that the punishment of demons and of impious men is only temporary…"

and it goes on to anathematize a future restoration (ἀποκατάστασις) of demons and impious men.

The tenth anathematizes "Origen and that Adamantius, who set forth these opinions" and all who hold them. The NPNF editor warns that the Greek and Latin of the text do not agree and that it may be corrupt.

**This is the plainest condemnation of universal restoration in the sources.** It names restoration, it names Origen, and it was accepted by the pope and patriarchs. What it lacks is the authority of an ecumenical council, and that is where the argument about binding force begins.

### 553: the fifteen anathemas

The Second Council of Constantinople, counted as the fifth ecumenical council, met from 5 May to 2 June 553 to condemn the Three Chapters (writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret and Ibas). Its Acts deal with the Three Chapters, not with Origenism.

Fifteen anathemas against Origenist teaching survive separately, found in a Vienna manuscript in the seventeenth century under the heading "of the 165 holy fathers of the holy fifth synod". Their first reads:

> "If anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema."

The rest condemn a system in which bodiless minds fall into bodies and become the ranks of angels, men and demons; Christ is a *nous* who never fell, united to the Word; all rational beings will one day be united "in one", with bodies, numbers and names abolished and "an identity of the gnosis and of the hypostasis"; the Kingdom of Christ will end; and "the end and the beginning shall be alike".

The scholarship on their status:

- **Not acts of the council.** Hefele held that they belonged to the 543 synod, while admitting "we certainly possess no strong and decisive proof". Norman Tanner's *Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils* (1990) omits them, saying "these anathemas cannot be attributed to this council".
- **Signed in 553, before the opening.** Franz Diekamp (1899) argued that Justinian had them drawn up and submitted to the bishops assembled in Constantinople before the council formally opened, perhaps in March or April 553 (Hombergen). This is now the standard view. Richard Price, the council's English translator (*The Acts of the Council of Constantinople of 553*, 2009, vol. 2, pp. 270–86), holds it, adding that "condemning Origenism was one of the activities that filled the bishops' time" while they waited for Vigilius, and that the council's authority over them was only questioned in modern times.
- **Passed by the council itself.** Noris and the Ballerini brothers in the eighteenth century, and Harnack, Möller and Loofs after them, held that the fifth council condemned Origen as Justinian wished. Their main evidence is Evagrius Scholasticus (c. 593), who says Origen received special anathemas at the council, and Cyril of Scythopolis, who reports "a common and universal anathema" against Origen after the council.

Augustine Casiday (in correspondence Kimel reports) and Kallistos Ware read the fifteen as one system rejected as a whole. On that reading, the first anathema condemns restoration *as the consequence of* pre-existence, and does not reach a universalism without it.

### Canon 11: Origen's name

The council's eleventh anathema, which certainly is in its Acts, condemns "Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen, together with their impious writings". Two arguments are made that Origen's name was added later. It comes last, out of chronological order, and it is missing from the parallel list in Justinian's *Homonoia* and from the version Vigilius later signed. Vincenzi in the nineteenth century, and today Ramelli (Brill *Encyclopedia of Early Christianity*, 2019), take it to be an interpolation. Ramelli adds that interpolation was already suspected at the Third Council of Constantinople. Others, including the NPNF editor, see "no possible reason to doubt" the name. Truglia also points to the fifth session, where the council refers to Origen as already anathematized, "even now", by the bishops and Vigilius.

### Later reception

The Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) describes the fifth synod as gathered "against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius". Nicaea II (787) repeats this. Misiarczyk (*Vox Patrum*, 2016) observes that these later councils repeated the earlier condemnation without re-examining it. In the West the *Decretum Gelasianum* had already listed Origen's works among those not to be received, though it allowed some to be read.

## 4. Do the condemned doctrines match what Origen wrote?

**The case that they don't.** Antoine Guillaumont edited the unexpurgated Syriac version of Evagrius's *Kephalaia Gnostica* (S2, *Patrologia Orientalis* 28.1, 1958) and, in a study of 1962, set the 553 anathemas against it. That work is now generally treated as a core text behind the 553 condemnations, and most specialists since read the fifteen as aimed at Evagrian Origenism. Brian Daley (*The Hope of the Early Church*) calls their target a radicalised Evagrian Christology and an apokatastasis going beyond anything Origen or Gregory of Nyssa held. Ramelli holds that what they condemn is apokatastasis embedded in transmigration and pre-existence, not Origen's own doctrine. Mark Edwards (*Origen against Plato*, 2002) argues that Origen's view of human nature is radically opposed to Platonism, and he and Panayiotis Tzamalikos deny that Origen taught the pre-existence of souls at all. It is a minority position, but a live one: Peter Martens, reviewing Mark Scott's *Journey Back to God*, faults Scott for treating pre-existence as Origen's without an extended answer to them. Henri Crouzel argued that Origen was never properly condemned at all. Misiarczyk adds a complication: the S2 text that Guillaumont relied on may itself be contaminated by sixth-century Origenism, and the milder S1 may be the original. Most Evagrius specialists disagree, because the surviving Greek fragments side with S2.

**The case that they do.** Origen's *On First Principles* does speak of rational beings created before the world whose love cooled, and of an end like the beginning ("the end is always like the beginning", 1.6.2). Jenkins, as Kimel reports him, holds that the fifteen anathemas draw directly on Origen's writings. Michael McClymond (*The Devil's Redemption*, 2018, and his review of Ramelli in *Theological Studies*, 2015) argues that Ramelli fails to keep Origen distinct from Gregory of Nyssa and other writers, overstates interpolation in the record, and passes over the early non-universalists. Ramelli replied in the same journal.

**The textual problem underneath both.** *On First Principles* survives complete only in Rufinus's Latin, which by his own account softened passages. The Greek fragments come largely from Justinian's letter and other hostile sources, which may sharpen them. So both sides are, in part, arguing about which mediator to trust.

**Origen's own hedging.** Origen often presents his views on the end as inquiry rather than doctrine. In *Against Celsus* 6.26 he suggests that eternal punishment is taught to the many as a deterrent, without saying that it is untrue. He denied, in the letter quoted above, that the devil would be saved.

## 5. Was apokatastasis as such condemned?

**Yes.** The 543 ninth anathema condemns any view that the punishment of demons and impious men "is only temporary" and ends in restoration, with no condition about pre-existence. Pope and patriarchs signed it, and the 553 bishops endorsed a text against "the monstrous restoration". The Orthodox Church has long read the fifth council as having settled the matter, and reception, on this view, is what makes a council's teaching binding. This is Jenkins's position as Kimel reports it, and that of Truglia and McClymond.

**No, not as such.** The 543 edict has imperial and local authority only. The fifteen anathemas are not in the council's Acts, and the first condemns restoration as the consequence of pre-existence. Gregory of Nyssa taught universal restoration without pre-existence, was never condemned, and is venerated as a saint and a father. This is the view of Kimel, Hart, Ramelli, and in a more cautious form Ware and Daley. Kimel also argues that the appeal to reception is circular, and that a condemnation which does not specify what it condemns is of no use as dogma.

**A narrower historical answer.** Without taking the theological question, historians generally agree that in 553 the emperor and the bishops in Constantinople meant to condemn the Origenism of their own day, eschatology included, and that Origen's name was condemned with it. The disputed questions are whether that act binds the church as an ecumenical definition, and whether it touches a universalism that does without the Evagrian cosmology. Those are questions for theology, and the traditions answer them differently.

## 6. How binding, and in which traditions

- **Eastern Orthodox:** Constantinople II is ecumenical. The council's eleventh anathema names Origen, and Orthodox liturgical and canonical tradition has treated him as condemned. Whether the fifteen anathemas carry ecumenical authority, and whether universalism stays an opinion that may be held (a *theologoumenon*), is argued openly among Orthodox writers today: Jenkins and Truglia against, Kimel, Hart and in part Ware for.
- **Roman Catholic:** Constantinople II is counted among the ecumenical councils. The standard critical edition followed by Tanner leaves the fifteen anathemas out. Origen has never been canonised. Benedict XVI's general audiences of 25 April and 2 May 2007 called him "a figure crucial to the whole development of Christian thought" and a true master ("maestro"), and did not mention any condemnation.
- **Protestant and academic:** the question is mainly historical. Patristic scholarship since the mid-twentieth century has largely followed Guillaumont, Crouzel and Daley in separating Origen from sixth-century Origenism. McClymond is the most prominent recent voice against that consensus where it touches universalism.

## 7. Was it justified?

That is a judgment the sources can't make, but the arguments on either side can be set out.

- **Against:** Cyril Richardson ("The Condemnation of Origen", *Church History*, 1937) called it "one of the saddest episodes in the history of the Christian church": a church condemning distorted passages and losing the breadth of early Greek theology. John of Jerusalem's objection, that a man who died in the peace of the church could not be retroactively condemned, was made in Origen's own century. Others stress the politics: Theophilus's war on the monks, Justinian's need to hold the Palestinian monasteries and the West, and an edict apparently drafted from hostile excerpts. Kimel quotes Baghos calling Origen a sacrifice "on the altar of imperial politics".
- **For:** the sixth-century system really did teach things every party now rejects, such as minds becoming equal to Christ and the end of Christ's kingdom. Origen's speculation in *On First Principles* plausibly supplied its starting points. On this view the church was right to rule the system out, and condemning its fountainhead was a defensible way to do it. Price notes that nobody questioned the council's authority in this matter before modern times.

## 8. Where the debate stands now (2024–26)

Hart put it most bluntly in "Saint Origen" (*First Things*, October 2015): the oldest records show the fifteen anathemas "were never even discussed by the assembled bishops", Origen "is never named in any of them", and even if approved they would condemn only a version of apokatastasis tied to one account of pre-existence. Hart also says they were attached to the council's canons well after it closed, which goes further than Diekamp and Price, who hold that the same bishops signed them in 553 before the opening. *That All Shall Be Saved* (2019) makes the theological case at length.

The most visible exchange is between Dr Cyril Jenkins (PhD; director of the St Basil Center for Orthodox Thought and Culture at Eastern University, and host of the Ancient Faith podcast *Light Through the Past*), who argued in an interview for Ancient Faith Ministries that the fifth council condemned apokatastasis and that its anathemas bind the Orthodox Church, and Fr Aidan Kimel, whose open letter of 20 August 2024 (*Eclectic Orthodoxy*) answers him point by point from the primary texts. Kimel engages Crouzel, Daley, Hombergen, Ramelli, Ware, Tanner, Hefele, Daniélou and others. Craig Truglia (*Orthodox Christian Theology*, 2019 and 2021) argues the condemnation side against Kimel and Hart, appealing to Price's translation and the fifth-session text. Kimel has since carried the argument further in a series on apokatastasis and the fifth council (part 3, January 2026). It answers Ignatius Green, who argues (2019, introducing Gregory of Nyssa's *Catechetical Discourse*) that the council implicitly approved the anathemas, and Price's conjecture that they dropped out of the Latin Acts. It also weighs J. A. McGuckin's interpolation hypothesis (2017), Georgi Maximov (*The Orthodox Word*, 2020) and Marius Telea (*International Journal of Orthodox Theology*, 2022), the last two on the traditional side. In the academy Ramelli and McClymond remain the poles on universalism, and Price's translation of the Acts is the shared reference for both sides.

**Gaps in this survey.** The Jenkins interview itself could not be found online (it isn't among the listed episodes of his podcast), so his position is still given as Kimel reports it; his credentials are now confirmed from his Ancient Faith profile. Guillaumont, Edwards and Clark are reported from secondary sources, the publisher and a scholarly review, not from the books. Page references to Price come from secondary citation.
