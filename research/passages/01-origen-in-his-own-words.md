# Origen in his own words

Most of what Origen wrote is lost. The whole of *On First Principles* (in Greek *Peri Archon*), the book his critics always went back to, survives only in Rufinus's Latin of 398, which Rufinus admits he smoothed over, and hostile readers such as Jerome and Justinian quoted other versions. Which text you trust goes a long way toward deciding what Origen taught. Some of his other works do survive in his own Greek, and they don't carry those doubts. This file gives the passages scholars on both sides cite most.

## What these texts show

Origen's own words can be set beside the later quarrels, but nearly everything in his central book, On First Principles, survives only in Rufinus's Latin, and Rufinus says he smoothed passages he believed heretics had corrupted. Read in that Latin, the Preface lists, among the points plainly handed down through the apostles' preaching, eternal life for the saved and eternal fire for the lost (free will is then called settled), and leaves open where the soul comes from, what the devil and angels are, and what came before and will come after this world. Chapter 1.6.1-2 offers the idea that the end is like the beginning as inquiry rather than definition, and says between its two quoted sentences that punishment according to sins comes before the end; 2.10.4-6 pictures punishment as a physician's fire that mends a dissolved soul; and 3.6 describes a restoration that comes in stages, with the body changed rather than destroyed. Against Celsus, which survives in Greek, says in 6.26 that the full account of punishment is not for the many, and in 8.72 that the Word will one day master every rational soul and abolish evil, while setting aside the question whether evil could ever arise again. Homilies on Jeremiah 20 (also Greek) defends a God who ‘deceives’ for good and speaks of a ‘mystery’ the many must not learn; the lines quoted do not say what the hidden truth about punishment is, though the example just before them (a twice-married woman who has ‘a share in some salvation’) suggests it is milder than the ‘eternal’ punishment the many are told of. The Commentary on Romans (Rufinus's abridged Latin) says in 5.10 that Christ's cross suffices for past, present and future ages and that love, which never fails, will keep the whole creation from falling, and in 6.5 that ‘eternal’ can mean a lifetime or this present age, that eternal life with the Lord has no end, and that even those who rise to shame will have ‘eternal life’, though not in Christ Jesus. Where Rufinus and the hostile Greek in Justinian's Letter to Menas (543) differ, as at 1.2.13, editors split: Koetschau (and so Butterworth, who translated his text) trust the Greek and print a mixed text, while Crouzel-Simonetti and Behr print Rufinus and move the Greek aside. Scholars read the lines in different directions: Ramelli (through Nemes, Sytsma and Kimel, and in her own review of Arruzza), Hannen and Heide find a restoration that is taught; Crouzel (through Sytsma and Hannen) calls it a hope that cannot be certain without denying freedom; Batiffol finds Origen teaching it as a rule but at times reluctant to decide and not wishing it preached to all; McClymond doubts both Rufinus's Latin and Ramelli's defence; and Martens reads the Preface as making the church's rule of faith the test. Caution on method: the Latin of On First Principles and of the Romans commentary below is Migne's (Patrologia Graeca 11 and 14, Delarue's text) read from OCR page transcriptions, not Koetschau's or Hammond Bammel's critical text, which could not be reached.

### 1. The rule of faith, and what the church's preaching leaves open (On First Principles, Preface 4-10)

**Source:** Origen, On First Principles (Peri Archon, De principiis), Preface 4-10, in Rufinus's Latin translation · **Date:** Origen's Greek: written in Alexandria, before c. 230 (exact date disputed). Rufinus's Latin: 397-98.  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This is Origen's own list of what the church's preaching has settled and what it has not. In Rufinus's Latin, eternal fire (‘igni aeterno’) stands on the list of points ‘plainly handed down’ through the apostles' preaching (section 4 introduces the list, section 5 gives the soul's reward and punishment), and free will is then added as ‘settled’ (definitum); what Greek word lies behind ‘aeterno’ is not known. The origin of the soul, the nature of the devil and angels, and the time before and after this world are not on it. Later condemnations (the 553 anathemas) treat some of those open questions, pre-existence above all, as heresy, so the argument is over whether Origen offered them as inquiry or taught them as doctrine.

**Latin (Rufinus's translation; the Greek of the Preface is lost)**

> (4) Species vero eorum quae per praedicationem apostolicam manifeste traduntur, istae sunt. Primo quod unus Deus est qui omnia creavit atque composuit … Tum deinde honore ac dignitate Patri ac Filio sociatum tradiderunt Spiritum sanctum. In hoc non jam manifeste discernitur, utrum natus an innatus vel Filius etiam Dei ipse habendus sit, necne. Sed inquirenda jam ista pro viribus sunt de sacra Scriptura, et sagaci perquisitione investiganda.
>
> (5) Post haec jam quod anima substantiam vitamque habens propriam, cum ex hoc mundo discesserit, pro suis meritis dispensabitur, sive vitae aeternae ac beatitudinis haereditate potitura, si hoc ei sua gesta praestiterint; sive igni aeterno ac suppliciis mancipanda … Est et illud definitum in ecclesiastica praedicatione, omnem animam rationabilem esse liberi arbitrii et voluntatis … De anima vero utrum ex seminis traduce ducatur, ita ut ratio ipsius vel substantia inserta ipsis seminibus corporalibus habeatur, an vero aliud habeat initium: et hoc ipsum initium si genitum est, aut non genitum: vel certe si extrinsecus corpori inditur, necne: non satis manifesta praedicatione distinguitur.
>
> (6) De diabolo et angelis ejus contrariisque virtutibus ecclesiastica praedicatio docuit, quoniam sunt quidem haec; quae autem sint, aut quomodo sint, non satis clare exposuit.
>
> (7) … Quid tamen ante hunc mundum fuerit, aut quid post mundum erit, jam non pro manifesto multis innotuit.
>
> (10) … De sole autem et luna et stellis utrum animantia sint, an sine anima, manifeste non traditur.

**English** (Own translation. For comparison: F. Crombie in the Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 4 (1885, public domain); G. W. Butterworth (1936); J. Behr (2017). None was opened for this entry. Contested renderings: ‘aeternus’ (eternal or age-long) in ‘igni aeterno’ and ‘vita aeterna’; ‘natus an innatus’ (born/unborn, or begotten/unbegotten, or made/unmade, see the note); ‘praedicatio ecclesiastica’ (the church's preaching, proclamation or teaching).)

> (4) The main points of what is plainly handed down through the apostles' preaching are these. First, that there is one God, who created and arranged all things … Then they handed down that the Holy Spirit is joined with the Father and the Son in honour and dignity. About this it is not yet clearly distinguished whether he is born or unborn, or whether he is also to be counted a Son of God, or not. But these matters must be inquired into, as far as we are able, from the sacred Scripture, and tracked down by a keen search.
>
> (5) Next, that the soul, having its own substance and life, when it leaves this world will be dealt with according to its merits: either to inherit eternal life and blessedness, if its deeds have earned it, or to be handed over to eternal fire and punishments … It is also settled in the church's preaching that every rational soul has free choice and will … But as to the soul, whether it is passed on by descent, so that its principle or substance is contained in the bodily seeds themselves, or whether it has some other beginning, and whether that beginning is itself begotten or unbegotten, or again whether it is put into the body from outside or not: this is not distinguished with sufficient clarity in the preaching.
>
> (6) About the devil and his angels and the opposing powers, the church's preaching has taught that they exist; what they are or how they exist it has not set out clearly enough.
>
> (7) … But what there was before this world, or what will be after the world, has not yet become plain to many.
>
> (10) … As to the sun, moon and stars, whether they are living beings or without soul, nothing is plainly handed down.

**Edition:** Standard critical edition: P. Koetschau, Origenes Werke 5: De principiis (Peri archon), Griechische christliche Schriftsteller (GCS) 22 (Leipzig: Hinrichs, 1913), Praefatio 4-10; page not confirmed. Modern editions with translation: H. Crouzel and M. Simonetti, Origene: Traite des principes, Sources Chretiennes (SC) 252 (Paris: Cerf, 1978); J. Behr, Origen: On First Principles, Oxford Early Christian Texts (Oxford: OUP, 2017). Neither was opened.

**About the text:** Migne's apparatus (cols. 117-120) records the manuscript readings Delarue preferred over the earlier printed editions. The most telling is note 51: where Rufinus has ‘natus an innatus’ (begotten or unbegotten) for the Spirit, Jerome's Letter 94 (= 124) to Avitus renders it ‘factus an infectus’ (made or unmade); Delarue reasons that Jerome read the Greek with one nu (genetos/agenetos, created/uncreated) and Rufinus with two (gennetos/agennetos, begotten/unbegotten). That one-letter difference decides whether Origen is asking if the Spirit is a creature. Note 55: the manuscripts read ‘extrinsecus corpori inditur’ (put in from outside) where some earlier editions had ‘intrinsecus’. Notes 53-54: a clause of section 5 is also found in the Apology for Origen by Pamphilus (in Rufinus's Latin), which is one of the few controls on Rufinus. Rufinus's own preface (Jerome Letter 80) says he removed or corrected passages he thought heretics had inserted; the c400 file in this survey has that text. Section numbers follow Migne/Delarue; they agree with Koetschau's in the Preface as far as I could tell, not confirmed.

### 2. ‘The end is always like the beginning’ (On First Principles 1.6.1-2)

**Source:** Origen, On First Principles 1.6.1-2 (Rufinus's Latin); the sentence is cited as 1.6.2 · **Date:** Origen's Greek before c. 230; Rufinus's Latin 397-98  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

These lines are the source of the famous sentence that the end is like the beginning, which the 553 fifteen anathemas echo (‘the end and the beginning shall be alike’). The argument is over what ‘beginning’ means (a state of bodiless pre-existent minds, or only God's original purpose) and how firmly the surrounding words claim it: Origen frames it as discussion ‘rather than definition’.

**Latin (Rufinus's translation; no Greek survives for these lines)**

> (1.6.1) … Quae quidem a nobis etiam cum magno metu et cautela dicuntur, discutientibus magis et pertractantibus quam pro certo ac definito statuentibus. … Nunc autem disputandi specie magis quam definiendi, prout possumus, exercemur. Finis ergo mundi et consummatio dabitur, cum unusquisque pro merito peccatorum etiam poenis subjicietur; quod tempus Deus solus agnoscit, quando unusquisque quod meretur expendet. In unum sane finem putamus quod bonitas Dei per Christum suum universam revocet creaturam, subactis et subditis etiam inimicis.
>
> (1.6.2) Talem igitur finem videntes, cum omnes inimici subjecti erunt Christo, et cum novissimus inimicus destruetur mors, et cum tradetur a Christo cui omnia subjecta sunt, regnum Deo Patri, ab isto, inquam, tali fine rerum contemplemur initia. Semper enim similis est finis initiis; et ideo sicut unus omnium finis, ita unum omnium intelligi debet initium; et sicut multorum unus finis, ita ab uno initio multae differentiae ac varietates, quae rursum per bonitatem Dei et subjectionem Christi, atque unitatem Spiritus sancti in unum finem qui sit initio similis revocantur.

**English** (Own translation. Standard comparisons: Crombie (ANF 4, 1885, public domain); Butterworth (1936); Behr (2017), none opened. Contested renderings: ‘finis’ (end, goal or purpose); ‘revocet’ and ‘revocantur’ (call back, recall, restore; the Greek verb behind them is not preserved); ‘putamus’ (we think, i.e. a hedged claim); ‘initiis’ is plural (beginnings); ‘universam creaturam’ (the whole creation, which may or may not include the devil and demons: Rufinus and Jerome disagree).)

> (1.6.1) … We say these things with great fear and caution, discussing and weighing them rather than laying them down as certain and defined. … Here, as well as we can, we are exercising ourselves in the manner of a debate rather than of a definition. The end and consummation of the world will be given when each person is made subject to punishments in proportion to his sins; God alone knows that time, when each will pay what he deserves. We think that the goodness of God, through his Christ, will call back his whole creation into one end, with even the enemies conquered and subdued.
>
> (1.6.2) Seeing such an end, when all enemies shall be subject to Christ, when the last enemy, death, shall be destroyed, and when the kingdom is handed over by Christ, to whom all things are subject, to God the Father: from that end, I say, let us consider the beginnings. For the end is always like the beginnings; and therefore, as we must understand one end of all things, so also one beginning of all; and as there is one end of many, so from one beginning come many differences and varieties, which are in turn called back, through God's goodness, the subjection of Christ and the unity of the Holy Spirit, into one end that is like the beginning.

**Edition:** P. Koetschau, GCS 22 (Leipzig, 1913); Hilberg's source notes (as quoted in the c400 file) put 1.6.2-3 at pp. 81-83, which I did not check myself. Crouzel-Simonetti, SC 252 (1978); Behr (OUP, 2017): not opened.  
**Checked against:** NOT Koetschau. Migne, Patrologia Graeca 11, cols. 165-166 (Delarue's text), from the Fabio3rs/BibliothecaPatristica OCR transcription (teste/PG011, first batch, page 087), which separates the main text from Delarue's notes. Ligatures written out; no other change. Second pass: all other words re-found in the same page (after joining hyphenated line-ends); Delarue’s notes 51, 54, 57, 58, 60, 61, 62 and 64 read as described. ([source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Fabio3rs/BibliothecaPatristica/34c5de5a1e066fe518744e5dc34b219afa56098a/teste/PG011/text/4adc5a68-de09-46f3-a342-d650969dfb6f-087.txt))

**About the text:** Delarue marks the key sentences ‘Cautè lege’ (read with caution) in notes 57, 60, 61 and 64 (note 58, on ‘In unum sane finem putamus’, only sends the reader to Huet’s Origeniana, 2.11.9 and 2.35.30) and, in note 62, says that Jerome (Letter 94 = 124 to Avitus) attacks this very place. Note 51: some earlier editions had ‘videtur esse judicium’ where the manuscripts have ‘videtur indicium’ (a sign). Note 54: the manuscripts read ‘fidem ecclesiasticam’ where earlier editions had ‘catholicam’. The word ‘putamus’ (we think) in ‘In unum sane finem putamus’ is Rufinus's; if his Greek had a stronger verb, this is where he could have softened it, which is the kind of question the c400 file raises about 1.6.3.

**How scholars read it**

- **Jerome (ancient reader)**, Letter 94 (also numbered 124) to Avitus, quoted in Delarue's note 62 at PG 11 col. 166. Reads 1.6.2 as teaching an endless cycle: ‘the beginning is born again from the end and the end from the beginning; and so all things change, so that he who is now a man could become a demon in another world; and a demon who has acted rather carelessly is banished into coarser bodies, that is, becomes a man. So he mixes everything up, so that from an angel the devil can come to be and the devil return to being an angel.’ (Own translation of the Latin as printed in the note.) (*ancient hostile reader: takes ‘end like beginning’ to mean endless cycling between angel, man and devil*; read in the scholar's own words; [source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Fabio3rs/BibliothecaPatristica/34c5de5a1e066fe518744e5dc34b219afa56098a/teste/PG011/text/4adc5a68-de09-46f3-a342-d650969dfb6f-087.txt))
- **Michael McClymond**, ‘Origenes Vindicatus vel Rufinus Redivivus’, Theological Studies 76.4 (2015), p. 824 n. 24. Quotes ‘the end is always like the beginning’ to show that Origen's apokatastasis (restoration of all things) is a return to a state that existed before, and contrasts this with Gregory of Nyssa's rejection of pre-existent souls. His wider charge is that Ramelli does not keep Origen apart from Gregory of Nyssa. (*reads the sentence as tied to Origen's pre-existence, so not a model for Nyssa's universalism*; from a reliable report of the scholar's view; [source](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/7-McClymond-Origenes-Vindicatus.pdf))

In n. 13 (p. 197) he adds that Origen takes a more cautious line on the devil: in a letter he denies teaching the devil’s redemption, but in his dialogue with Candidus he admits it as a real possibility (he cites Jennifer Heckart, Union Seminary Quarterly Review 60). (*reads 1.6 as teaching restoration of creation, with the devil’s case left open*; from a reliable report of the scholar's view; [source](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/dionysius/article/view/6482/5688))
- **Henri Crouzel (as reported by Chris Hannen)**, Hannen, ‘Rob Bell and Origen’, Stimulus 23.2 (year not confirmed), p. 24 and nn. 49-50 (Crouzel, pp. 163-168). Hannen cites Crouzel for calling On First Principles “research theology”. The words “in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than in that of fixed and certain decision” are Hannen quoting Origen in an English translation, cited to 1.6.1 (n. 50), not Crouzel’s own words. (*reads 1.6 as inquiry that does not claim to define*; from a reliable report of the scholar's view; [source](https://www.laidlaw.ac.nz/assets/Files-PDF-Word/10036-Stimulus-Vol-23-Is-2_Hannen.pdf))


### 3. Punishment as a physician's fire (On First Principles 2.10.4-6)

**Source:** Origen, On First Principles 2.10.4-6 (Rufinus's Latin) · **Date:** Origen's Greek before c. 230; Rufinus's Latin 397-98  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

Here Origen most clearly describes punishment after death as medicine: the fire is the soul's own inflamed sins, and God uses it to rebuild what has come apart. Whether that means punishment ends (and so restoration) is the question the sixth-century anathema on ‘temporary punishment’ answers with a condemnation; Origen himself in these sections does not say how long it lasts.

**Latin (Rufinus's translation; no Greek survives for these sections)**

> (2.10.4) … ita anima cum multitudinem malorum operum et abundantiam in se congregaverit peccatorum, competenti tempore omnis illa malorum congregatio effervescit ad supplicium, atque inflammatur ad poenas; … tunc et ipsa conscientia propriis stimulis agitatur atque compungitur, et sui ipsa efficitur accusatrix et testis. …
>
> (2.10.5) … Quae animae dissolutio atque divulsio cum adhibiti ignis ratione fuerit explorata, sine dubio ad firmiorem sui compaginem instaurationemque solidatur.
>
> (2.10.6) Multa sunt etiam alia quae nos latent, quae illi soli cognita sunt, qui est medicus animarum nostrarum. … quanto magis intelligendum est et hunc medicum nostrum Deum volentem diluere vitia animarum nostrarum, quae ex peccatorum et scelerum diversitate collegerant, ut huiuscemodi poenalibus curis, insuper etiam ignis inferre supplicium his qui sanitatem animae perdiderunt? … Ex quo utique intelligitur quod furor vindictae Dei ad purgationem proficit animarum. Quoniam autem et ea poena quae per ignem inferri dicitur, pro adjutorio intelligitur adhiberi, Isaias docet …

**English** (Own translation. Standard comparisons: Crombie (ANF 4, 1885, public domain); Butterworth (1936); Behr (2017), none opened. Contested renderings: ‘furor vindictae’ (fury or anger of vengeance; the Latin may reflect Greek orge); ‘purgationem’ (purging or purification); ‘ignem’ is the fire of punishment but Origen treats it as the soul's own passions in 2.10.4-5, so ‘fire’ is partly figurative.)

### 4. God ‘all in all’: restoration in stages, and the body changed, not destroyed (On First Principles 3.6)

**Source:** Origen, On First Principles 3.6.1, 3.6.3 and 3.6.6 (Rufinus's Latin), with a Greek fragment at 3.6.3 preserved by Justinian · **Date:** Origen's Greek before c. 230; Rufinus's Latin 397-98  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This chapter is Origen’s fullest account of the end. Three points are in dispute between readers: whether ‘all in all’ includes the wicked and the devil, whether the stages of amendment end with every rational being restored, and whether bodies belong to the final state. Rufinus’s Latin has this body changed into a spiritual body, not abolished; the Greek fragment Justinian quotes in his Letter to Menas (see the textual notes) says God does not become ‘all in all’ in bodies, which by their own nature are lifeless.

**Latin (Rufinus), with one Greek fragment (Justinian's Letter to Menas, 543)**

> (3.6.1) … Igitur summum bonum ad quod natura rationabilis universa festinat, quod etiam finis omnium dicitur, a quamplurimis etiam philosophorum hoc modo terminatur, quia summum bonum sit, prout possibile est, similem fieri Deo. …
>
> (3.6.3) … quod non solum in omnibus esse dicitur Deus, sed etiam omnia in omnibus. Quae sint ergo ista omnia quae Deus futurus sit in omnibus, requiramus. …
>
> (3.6.6) … cum sensim et per singulos emendatio fuerit et correctio prosecuta, praecurrentibus aliis et velociori cursu ad summa tendentibus, aliis vero proximo quoque spatio insequentibus, tum deinde aliis longe posterius, et sic per multos et innumeros ordines proficientium ac Deo se ex inimicis reconciliantium pervenitur usque ad novissimum inimicum qui dicitur mors, ut etiam ipse destruatur ne ultra sit inimicus. Cum ergo restitutae fuerint omnes rationabiles animae in huiuscemodi statum, tunc natura etiam huius corporis nostri in spiritalis corporis gloriam perducetur. … non aliud corpus est quo nunc in ignobilitate et in corruptione et infirmitate utimur, et aliud erit illud quo in incorruptione, et in virtute, et in gloria utemur; sed hoc idem abjectis his infirmitatibus in quibus nunc est, in gloriam transmutabitur spiritale effectum, ut quod fuit indignitatis vas, hoc ipsum expurgatum fiat vas honoris et beatitudinis habitaculum.

**English** (Own translation. Standard comparisons: Crombie (ANF 4, 1885, public domain); Butterworth (1936); Behr (2017), none opened. Contested renderings: ‘restitutae’ (restored; the Greek is apokatastasis-language, so ‘restoration’ and ‘universal salvation’ are the two usual glosses); ‘destruatur’ (destroyed, abolished or put out of action: the point is whether the enemy ceases to exist or ceases to be an enemy); ‘Deus omnia in omnibus’ (1 Cor 15:28: God all in all, or God everything in everyone); ‘rationabiles animae’ (rational souls or rational beings).)

> (3.6.1) … The highest good, toward which all rational nature hurries, which is also called the end of all things, is defined by very many of the philosophers too in this way: that the highest good is to become like God, as far as is possible. …
>
> (3.6.3) … that God is said to be not only in all things but also all things in all. Let us ask, then, what those ‘all things’ are that God will be in all. …
>
> (3.6.6) … when, little by little and one by one, amendment and correction have gone forward — some running ahead and reaching the heights at a swifter pace, others following close behind, others again much later — and so through many and countless ranks of those advancing and being reconciled to God from being enemies, one comes at last to the last enemy, which is called death, so that even he is destroyed and is no longer an enemy. When, then, all rational souls have been restored to such a state, then the nature of this body of ours too will be carried up into the glory of the spiritual body. … It is not one body that we now use in dishonour, corruption and weakness, and another that we shall use in incorruption, power and glory; rather this same body, its present weaknesses cast off, will be changed into glory, made spiritual, so that what was a vessel of dishonour, once purified, becomes a vessel of honour and a dwelling of blessedness.

**Edition:** P. Koetschau, GCS 22 (Leipzig, 1913), Book 3 chapter 6; page not confirmed. Crouzel-Simonetti, SC 268 (1980; volume number from memory); Behr (OUP, 2017): not opened.

### 5. Where Rufinus and Justinian's Greek disagree: the Son as ‘image of God's goodness’ (On First Principles 1.2.13)

**Source:** Origen, On First Principles 1.2.13: Rufinus's Latin, and a Greek fragment preserved at the end of Justinian's Letter to Menas (543; Patrologia Graeca 86.1, Mansi 9) · **Date:** Origen's Greek before c. 230; Rufinus 397-98; Justinian 543  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This is the clearest small case of the editions problem, and Peter Martens uses it for that purpose. If the Greek is Origen's, Rufinus has made the Son's goodness equal to the Father's; if Rufinus is right, Justinian's quotation is doctored or taken out of context. It is not about universal salvation itself, but every dispute over what Origen taught on the end turns on the same prior question: trust Rufinus's Latin, or the hostile Greek? The same fork appears at 3.6.3 (the Greek fragment on God ‘all in all’ above).

**Latin (Rufinus) and Greek (Justinian's quotation, as Migne prints it)**

> RUFINUS:
>
> (1.2.13) Superest quid sit imago bonitatis ejus inquirere: in quo eadem, ut opinor, intelligi convenit quae superius de imagine ea quae per speculum deformatur, expressimus. [Greek fragment inserted here by Migne, see below] Principalis namque bonitas sine dubio, ex qua Filius natus; qui per omnia imago est Patris, procul dubio etiam boni[…] talis ejus convenienter imago dicetur. Non enim alia aliqua secunda bonitas exsistit in Filio praeter eam quae est in Patre. Unde et recte ipse Salvator in Evangelio dicit quoniam « nemo bonus nisi unus Deus Pater »; quo scilicet per hoc intelligatur Filius non esse alterius bonitatis, sed illius solius quae in Patre est; cujus, recte imago appellatur, quia neque aliunde est, nisi ex ipsa principali bonitate; ne altera bonitas quam ea quae in Patre est, videatur in Filio. Neque aliqua dissimilitudo aut distantia bonitatis in Filio est. || JUSTINIAN (Greek, as printed in PG 11, col. 143-144, note 56): Οὗτω τοίνυν ἡγοῦμαι· καὶ ἐπὶ τοῦ Σωτῆρος καλῶς ἂν λεχθήσεσθαι ὅτι εἰκὼν ἀγαθότητος τοῦ Θεοῦ ἐστιν, ἀλλ’ οὐκ αὐτὸ ἀγαθόν· καὶ τάχα καὶ Υἱὸς ἀγαθός, ἀλλ’ οὐχ ὡς ἁπλῶς ἀγαθός. Καὶ ὥσπερ εἰκὼν ἐστι τοῦ Θεοῦ τοῦ ἀοράτου, καὶ κατὰ τοῦτο Θεός, ἀλλ’ οὐ περὶ οὗ λέγει αὐτὸς ὁ Χριστός· ‘ἵνα γινώσκωσί σε τὸν μόνον ἀληθινὸν Θεόν’, οὕτως εἰκὼν ἀγαθότητος, ἀλλ’ οὐχ ὡς ὁ Πατὴρ ἀπαραλλάκτως ἀγαθός.

**English** (Own translation of the Latin and of the whole Greek sentence. Martens's gloss of the end of the first Greek sentence, ‘not purely and simply good’, agrees with ἁπλῶς in the scan (page of Martens's gloss not confirmed). ἀπαραλλάκτως is rendered ‘without any variation’ (Delarue’s own Latin version has ‘sine ulla differentia’). Standard translations: Butterworth (1936), Behr (2017), neither opened. Contested rendering: ‘ἀλλ᾿ οὐκ αὐτὸ ἀγαθόν’ (not goodness itself) against Rufinus's ‘no other goodness’: the first makes the Son's goodness derived and lesser, the second says it is the Father's goodness.)

> RUFINUS:
>
> (1.2.13) It remains to ask what the image of his goodness is; in this, I think, the same must be understood as we set out above about the image formed in a mirror. For the primary goodness is, without doubt, that from which the Son is born, who in everything is the image of the Father, and so without doubt also [… uncertain join] he will fittingly be called the image of such goodness of his. For no second goodness of any other kind exists in the Son besides the one that is in the Father. Hence the Saviour himself rightly says in the Gospel, ‘None is good but one, God the Father’, so that by this it may be understood that the Son is not of another goodness, but of that one goodness alone which is in the Father, whose image he is rightly called, because he is from nowhere else than from the primary goodness itself, lest another goodness than the one that is in the Father may seem to be in the Son. There is no unlikeness or distance of goodness in the Son. || JUSTINIAN’S GREEK: So, then, I think that of the Saviour too it would rightly be said that he is an image of God’s goodness, but not goodness itself; and perhaps also that the Son is good, but not good without qualification. And just as he is the image of the invisible God, and in that respect God, but not the one of whom Christ himself says, ‘That they may know you, the only true God’, so he is the image of goodness, but not, as the Father is, good without any variation.

### 6. Why the full teaching on punishment is not for the many (Against Celsus 6.26)

**Source:** Origen, Against Celsus (Contra Celsum) 6.26 · **Date:** c. 248, Caesarea  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This is a passage cited (by Batiffol and by Heide, among those read here) for the claim that Origen held a fuller doctrine than he taught the simple. He says the plain account of punishment is enough for the many and that going beyond it would not help those who are held back from evil by fear of an ‘eternal’ punishment. He does not say what lies beyond it, and he does not call the doctrine of eternal punishment false.

**Greek**

> Ὅσα δ᾿ εἰς τὸν τόπον λεχθείη ἂν, οὔτε πᾶσιν οὔτε τοῦ παρόντος καιροῦ ἐστι διηγήσασθαι· ἀλλ᾿ οὐδ᾿ ἀκίνδυνον τὴν τῶν τοιούτων σαφήνειαν πιστεῦσαι γραφῇ. ἅτε τῶν πολλῶν οὐ χρῃζόντων πλείονος διδασκαλίας παρὰ τὴν περὶ τῆς κατὰ τῶν ἁμαρτανόντων κολάσεως. εἰς γὰρ τὰ ὑπερέκεινα αὐτῆς οὐ χρήσιμον ἀναβαίνειν διὰ τοὺς μόγις φόβῳ τῆς αἰωνίου κολάσεως κἂν συστέλλοντας ἐπὶ ποσὸν τῆς κακίας καὶ τῶν ἀπ᾿ αὐτῆς ἁμαρτανομένων χύσιν.

**English** (Own translation. Compare ANF 4 (Crombie, public domain), whose English as shown on bkv.unifr.ch (translator not named there) has ‘nor to be uttered on the present occasion’, ‘not unattended with danger to commit to writing’ and ‘fear of eternal punishment’. Contested rendering: ‘αἰωνίου κολάσεως’ (eternal punishment; or age-long, lasting for an age, the meaning argued by Ramelli and Konstan for aionios, see the Romans 6.5 entry). Note that Origen himself uses the word here in the phrase the many fear, and does not say whether he thinks it is the right word. ‘τὸν τόπον’ (this subject, this place): the lines before it speak of purification by fire in Jerusalem, so the subject is the fire of punishment.)

> As for what might be said on this subject, it is neither for everyone nor for the present occasion to set it out; and it is not free of danger to entrust the plain statement of such matters to writing, since the many need no more teaching than the one about the punishment of sinners. To go beyond it is not useful, because of those who, barely, through fear of eternal punishment, hold back to some extent the outpouring of evil and of the sins that come from it.

Line numbers not given. Modern translation: H. Chadwick, Contra Celsum (Cambridge, 1953), not opened. Public-domain English: ANF 4 (Crombie), seen only in short phrases (below).

### 7. The Word will master every rational soul, and evil will be abolished (Against Celsus 8.72)

**Source:** Origen, Against Celsus (Contra Celsum) 8.72 · **Date:** c. 248, Caesarea  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This is a direct statement, in a late work (c. 248, by the date above), that the aim of the Word's healing is to abolish evil in every rational soul. He says it as what ‘we’ say, not as a guess. Then he refuses to decide, in this discussion, whether evil could ever return. Readers divide over how much weight the first half carries and how much the refusal carries.

**Greek**

> ἡμεῖς δὲ τῆς λογικῆς φύσεώς φαμεν ὅλης κρατῆσαί ποτε τὸν λόγον καὶ μεταποιῆσαι πᾶσαν ψυχὴν εἰς τὴν ἑαυτοῦ τελειότητα, ἐπὰν [289] ἕκαστος ψιλῇ χρησάμενος τῇ ἐξουσίᾳ ἕληται ἃ βούλεται καὶ γένηται ἐν οἷς εἵλατο· καὶ φαμεν ὅτι οὐκ ἔστιν εἰκὸς, ὥσπερ ἐπὶ τῶν ⟨ἐν⟩ τοῖς σώμασι νοσημάτων καὶ τραυμάτων τινὰ τῶν συμβαινόντων ἰσχυρότερα εἶναι πάσης ἰατρικῆς τέχνης, οὕτως ἐπὶ τῶν ψυχῶν εἶναί τι τῶν ἀπὸ κακίας ἀδύνατον ὑπὸ τοῦ ἐπὶ πᾶσι λογικοῦ καὶ θεοῦ θεραπευθῆναι. πάντων γὰρ τῶν ἐν ψυχῇ κακῶν δυνατώτερος ὢν ὁ λόγος καὶ ἡ ἐν αὐτῷ θεραπεία προσάγει κατὰ βούλησιν θεοῦ ἑκάστῳ αὐτὴν, καὶ τὸ τέλος τῶν πραγμάτων ἀναιρεθῆναί ἐστι τὴν κακίαν· πότερον δὲ ὥστε μηδαμῇ μηδαμῶς ἔτι αὐτὴν ἐπιτραπῆναι δύνασθαι ἢ μὴ, οὐ τοῦ παρόντος ἐστὶ λόγου διδάξαι.

**English** (Own translation. Compare ANF 4 (Crombie, public domain; as shown on bkv.unifr.ch, translator not named there): ‘The consummation of all things is the destruction of evil’ and ‘whether it shall be so destroyed that it can never anywhere arise again, it is beyond our present purpose to say’. Standard comparisons: Chadwick (1953); ANF 4 (public domain, Crombie); neither opened. Contested renderings: ‘κρατῆσαι’ (master, prevail over, gain control of); ‘μεταποιῆσαι’ (remake or transform); ‘ἀναιρεθῆναι τὴν κακίαν’ (that evil be abolished, taken away, destroyed); ‘ἐπιτραπῆναι’ in the last clause (be allowed, be given leave, be turned over: the sentence leaves open whether evil could ever be readmitted).)

> But we say that the Word will one day master the whole rational nature and remake every soul into his own perfection, when each, using only his own freedom, chooses what he wishes and becomes what he has chosen. And we say that, as with diseases and wounds of bodies some are stronger than every medical art, it is not likely that among souls anything arising from evil is incurable by the Word, who is over all, and by God. For the Word, being more powerful than all the evils in the soul, and the healing that is in him, brings it to each according to God's will; and the end of things is that evil is abolished. Whether so that it can in no way ever again be allowed [to arise], or not, is not for the present discussion to teach.

**Edition:** P. Koetschau, Origenes Werke 2, GCS 3 (Leipzig, 1899), pp. 288-289 (the bracketed [289] marks Koetschau's page break; ⟨ἐν⟩ is an editorial supplement (the digital text tags it as an addition and prints it in round brackets; I could not confirm from the printed page that it is Koetschau's own)). Chadwick (1953) and ANF 4 not opened.

### 8. ‘You deceived me, Lord’: the physician, the father and the hidden mystery (Homilies on Jeremiah 20.3-4)

**Source:** Origen, Homilies on Jeremiah, Homily 20, sections 3-4 (on Jeremiah 20:7) · **Date:** Caesarea, 240s (date uncertain)  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

With Against Celsus 6.26 this is where Origen most openly says that what the many are told about punishment may differ from what the wise are told. The disputed point is what the ‘truth about punishment’ is. Read closely, 20.3 says the hidden thing is that the baptised too must pass through punishment (the preceding lines say every sinner must be punished, and that his own ‘wood’ must be burned in him), not that all will be saved; 20.4 says some who found the truth took it for permission and did worse. The lines quoted do not themselves say what the truth is. The example given just before them (see the textual notes) suggests it is milder than the ‘eternal’ punishment the many fear, in the case of the twice-married, but the homily does not say that punishment ends for all.

**Greek**

> (20.3, p. 181) τὸ κατὰ τὸν τόπον μυστήριον κεκρύφθαι δεῖ, ἵνα μὴ ἐκκακήσῃ ὁ πολύς, ἵνα μὴ μαθὼν τὰ πράγματα προσδοκήσῃ τὴν ἔξοδον οὐχ ὡς ἀνάπαυσιν, ἀλλ᾿ ὡς κόλασιν.
>
> (20.3, p. 182) ἄλλη γὰρ ἡ ἀπάτη ἡ ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ, ἄλλη ἡ ἀπάτη ἡ ἀπὸ τοῦ ὄφεως.
>
> (20.4, p. 183) πόσοι δὲ νομιζόμενοι σοφοί, εὑρόντες τὸ περὶ κολάσεως ἀληθὲς καὶ διελθόντες δῆθεν τὰ τῆς ἀπάτης, ἐν χείρονι γεγόνασι βίῳ; ἐλυσιτέλει αὐτοὺς φρονεῖν, ὡς ἐφρόνουν πρότερον περὶ τοῦ ‘ὁ σκώληξ αὐτῶν οὐ τελευτήσει’ καὶ ὅτι ‘τὸ πῦρ αὐτῶν οὐ σβεσθήσεται’

**English** (Own translation. Contested renderings: ‘ἀπάτη’ and the verb of Jeremiah 20:7 (the Septuagint's ἠπάτησάς με: deceived, tricked; the Hebrew verb is commonly rendered ‘enticed’ or ‘persuaded’, which fits Origen's argument that the deception is for the person's good); ‘δῆθεν’ (supposedly, as they think); ‘τὸ περὶ κολάσεως ἀληθές’ (the truth about punishment: the lines quoted do not say what it is; see the textual notes for the example just before them).)

> (20.3) The mystery in this passage has to be kept hidden, so that the many may not lose heart, so that, learning how things are, they may not expect their departure as a rest but as a punishment.
>
> (20.3) For the deception from God is one thing, the deception from the serpent another.
>
> (20.4) How many who are thought wise, having found the truth about punishment and gone through, as they suppose, the business of the deception, have fallen into a worse way of life? It would have profited them to go on thinking as they used to think about ‘their worm shall not die’ and ‘their fire shall not be quenched’.

### 9. Love never fails: can restored beings fall again? (Commentary on Romans 5.10)

**Source:** Origen, Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans 5.10 (on Romans 6:9-10), Rufinus's abridged Latin translation; Patrologia Graeca 14, cols. 1053-1054 · **Date:** Origen's Greek c. 244 (dating uncertain); Rufinus's Latin c. 405-407  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

This is Origen's answer, as Rufinus gives it, to the objection that a creature with free choice could fall again even after being restored, so that the saving work would have to be repeated in each future age. Jerome claimed Origen's system ended in endless cycles of rise and fall (Letter 124); this passage says the opposite, that love will secure the creation, which is where it can be set against Jerome (this survey's own observation, not a scholar's). It also does not say that every being is already restored.

**Latin (Rufinus's abridged translation; no Greek survives for this part)**

> … potest fieri ut in quocunque statu fuerit anima, et in quacunque perfectione virtutum, quoniam virtus mutabilis est, lapsum possit incurrere … Haec ergo et his similia proponentes, easdem etiam in futuris saeculis dispensationes a Christo repetendas esse arbitrantur. Sed ad haec nos breviter prout possumus respondebimus. ### 10. What ‘eternal’ means: a lifetime, an age, or without end (Commentary on Romans 6.5)

**Source:** Origen, Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans 6.5 (on Romans 6:22-23), Rufinus's abridged Latin translation; Patrologia Graeca 14, cols. 1067-1068 · **Date:** Origen's Greek c. 244 (dating uncertain); Rufinus's Latin c. 405-407  
**How far it was checked:** V, checked against an edition or a scan of one.

Scholars who argue that Origen himself knew ‘eternal’ can mean a stretch of time point to the Romans commentary (Heide does, quoting a sentence of it). Here, in Rufinus's Latin, ‘in aeternum’ is said to mean a man's lifetime in one verse and the present age in another, while eternal life with the Lord is said to have no end. The sentence the draft left out adds that those who rise to shame will also have ‘eternal life’, though not ‘in Christ Jesus’, and that their reproach is ‘eternal’ too, so ‘aeternus’ is used of the lot of the lost as well as of the saved. The passage therefore supports more than one reading: that ‘eternal’ need not mean endless; that Origen holds the life of the saved to be endless; and that he applies the same word to both lots. How much weight it carries in the dispute over eternal punishment depends on whether the word may mean an age in one place and without end in another, and on how far Rufinus's Latin can be trusted to carry Origen's Greek.

**Latin (Rufinus's abridged translation; Greek for this passage not known to survive; the catena and papyrus fragments of the commentary were not checked)**

> … et erit tibi servus in aeternum. Aeternum hic sine dubio tempus vitae hominis posuit. Et iterum in Ecclesiaste dicitur: ‘Generatio vadit, et generatio venit, terra autem in aeternum stat’: hic aeternum praesentis saeculi tempus ostendit. Ubi vero dicit ‘vitam aeternam,’ ad illud aspiciendum est quod ipse Salvator dixit: ‘Haec est autem vita aeterna, ut cognoscant te solum verum Deum …’ … Sicut ergo semper cum Domino esse, finem non habet, ita et vita aeterna nullum finem habere credenda est. … Ego non puto etiam hoc vacuum esse, quod ad vitam aeternam addidit ‘in Christo [Jesu] Domino nostro’; sed quia fortassis sciri voluit aliud esse vitam aeternam solum, et aliud vitam aeternam in Christo Jesu. Et illi enim qui surgent in confusionem et opprobrium sempiternum, habebunt quidem vitam aeternam, non tamen in Christo Jesu, sed in confusione et opprobrio aeterno; justi vero qui surgent in vitam aeternam in Christo Jesu habebunt vitam aeternam.

**English** (Own translation. Standard English: Scheck (FC 104, 2002), not opened. The key word is Latin ‘aeternum’, presumably rendering Greek aionios (the Greek is lost; aion: an age; aionios: of the age, age-long, or, in later usage, eternal). Contested renderings: ‘in aeternum’ (for ever, for an age, for a lifetime); ‘vita aeterna’ (eternal life, or life of the age to come).)

> … and he will be your servant for ever [aeternum]. Here ‘for ever’ undoubtedly stands for the time of a man's life. And again, in Ecclesiastes it is said: ‘A generation goes and a generation comes, but the earth stands for ever’; here ‘for ever’ shows the time of the present age. But where he says ‘eternal life’, we must look to what the Saviour himself said: ‘This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God …’ … As, therefore, being always with the Lord has no end, so eternal life too must be believed to have no end. … I do not think even this is empty, that to ‘eternal life’ he added ‘in Christ [Jesus] our Lord’; perhaps he wanted it known that eternal life alone is one thing, and eternal life in Christ Jesus another. For even those who rise ‘to shame [confusionem] and everlasting [sempiternum] reproach’ will indeed have eternal life, though not in Christ Jesus: they will have it in shame and eternal [aeterno] reproach; but the righteous, who rise to eternal life in Christ Jesus, will have eternal life.

This entry dates Justinian's Letter to Menas to 543 and calls the later list the ‘553 anathemas’, as Heide does; the dates were not checked against a primary source. *Source:* Heide, Arc 44 (2016), p. 59 area; Batiffol, ‘Apocatastasis’, Catholic Encyclopedia vol. 1 (1907) (from a reliable report of the source's view)

## What couldn't be checked

- Koetschau's critical text of On First Principles (GCS 22, 1913) could not be read in sections; archive.org was reachable only through a tool that returns the first 59,000 characters or so. All Latin of On First Principles and of the Romans commentary is Migne's (Patrologia Graeca 11 and 14, Delarue's text) from OCR page images, so every status V on those passages means ‘checked against that printed edition’, not against Koetschau, Hammond Bammel, Crouzel-Simonetti or Behr. Spelling and some readings may differ there.
- OCR gaps: On First Principles 3.6.5 (the destruction of the last enemy as the end of its hostile will, not its substance) was not obtained, nor the middle of Rufinus's Latin at 3.6.2-3, and one stretch of the Latin on the 1.2.13 page remains open (marked in the entry). Ramelli's use of 3.6.5 is known only through her review of Arruzza.
- Justinian's Letter to Menas itself (PG 86.1, Mansi 9) was not opened; the Greek at 1.2.13 and 3.6.3 is as Migne prints it in PG 11, and was not checked against the letter.
- Butterworth, Behr, Crouzel-Simonetti, Hammond Bammel, Chadwick, Scheck and Smith were not opened. How Butterworth and Behr treat 1.2.13 is known only from Martens, Cavadini and Heide. The Vetus Latina volume numbers for the Romans commentary (16, 33, 34), the Sources Chrétiennes numbers for the Jeremiah homilies (232, 238) and the Fathers of the Church numbers (103, 104) are recalled from series lists, not confirmed.
- English renderings are my own throughout. Crombie's public-domain English (ANF 4) was compared only for Against Celsus 6.26 and 8.72, through bkv.unifr.ch; elsewhere it is named and was not opened, and no public-domain English exists for the Romans commentary or the Jeremiah homilies as far as I know.
- Homilies on Jeremiah 20: only one named scholarly reading was found (Somos's Hungarian introduction, read through short quotations, and the Hungarian translated by me). The neighbours (Batiffol, Heide and Greggs on Against Celsus 6.26) are about a different text.
- The readings for the Commentary on Romans 5.10 are partly about the problem the passage answers (freedom and certainty), not about 5.10 by name, and say so; Kimel's note quotes the passage without a location, and I matched it. For 6.5 no scholar who reads Origen's aionios as endless was read directly; McClymond's Jude 6 objection and the passage's own sentences are the nearest.
- Most scholars were read through a web fetch tool that returns segmented summaries with short quotations (Martens, Heide twice, McClymond, Sytsma, Hannen, Nemes, Cavadini, Batiffol, Kimel, Ramelli's review of Arruzza, Somos), so their statuses are S, not V. McClymond and Sytsma were read only for the footnotes and passages the tool reported; Sytsma's chapters on apocatastasis (pp. 134-180) were beyond the reachable text; the last 7,283 characters of Martens's article were not read.
- Hannen's journal year is not shown on the page (Stimulus 23.2; the page gives only an access date, 3 July 2015, in a footnote). The works of Greggs (cited at fn 81, p. 315) and Heine (fn 85, p. 256) behind Hannen's notes were not identified. Ramelli, Edwards, Tzamalikos, Daley, Heine, Scott, Ludlow and Hart were not read directly; Crouzel and Daley page references come from Hannen, Sytsma, Heide or Wikipedia.

- Dates (Origen's Commentary on Romans c. 244; Rufinus's translation c. 405-407; Justinian's Letter to Menas 543; the fifteen anathemas 553) are as the draft gave them and were not checked against a primary source; Heide and Batiffol disagree on the date of the anathemas (see other findings).
