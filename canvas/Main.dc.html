IV.

Was Origen condemned?

His name was, more than once. What the condemnations actually describe, and whether they reach universal salvation, is still argued. Here is the record, and who reads it which way.

  1. c. 231 Demetrius of Alexandria Local

    Two synods of his own bishop. The grievance was chiefly his ordination at Caesarea without leave, and the churches of Palestine ignored the sentence.

  2. c. 400 Theophilus of Alexandria, Pope Anastasius I Regional

    An Alexandrian synod condemns Origen and his books; Rome agrees. A fight over monks and church power as much as doctrine, and universal salvation is not the charge that carries it.

  3. 543 Justinian's edict Imperial

    Ten anathemas, endorsed by a home synod and signed by Pope Vigilius. The ninth condemns anyone who says the punishment of demons and the wicked is “only temporary” and ends in restoration. The tenth names Origen. The plainest text against universal salvation in the record.

  4. 553 The bishops at Constantinople Conciliar, disputed

    Fifteen anathemas against an Evagrian system, signed (most historians now hold) before the council formally opened, so they are not in its Acts. The council's eleventh anathema names Origen among the heretics; some scholars argue the name was added later.

  5. 680–787 Later councils Received

    Constantinople III and Nicaea II speak of the fifth council as gathered “against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius”. From here on, the tradition counts him condemned.

Was universal salvation condemned as such?

Yes

The 543 anathema needs no theory of pre-existence: it rules out any end to the punishment of the wicked. Pope Vigilius signed it, the 553 bishops condemned “the monstrous restoration”, and the church has received the fifth council as settling the matter.

Dr Cyril Jenkins (as reported by Kimel), Craig Truglia, Michael McClymond

Not as such

The 543 edict carries an emperor's authority, not a council's. The fifteen anathemas condemn restoration as the sequel to pre-existence, and Gregory of Nyssa, who taught it without pre-existence, was never condemned and is venerated as a saint.

Fr Aidan Kimel, David Bentley Hart, Ilaria Ramelli; more cautiously Kallistos Ware, Brian Daley

“If anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema.”

The first of the fifteen anathemas, 553

Where historians mostly agree: what the 553 texts describe is a sixth-century system that specialists trace to Evagrius of Pontus and his Palestinian followers, and Origen's name was condemned along with it. Whether that binds, and whether it touches a universalism without the Evagrian cosmology, the traditions answer differently.
